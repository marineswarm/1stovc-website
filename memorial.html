Thirteen men did not come home

Our 96-page research binder, Remember the Men, traces Company A through the original records. It ends with a memorial roll of the men who gave their lives in service.

Three of them — Allen, Larimer and J. L. Boggs — were found through their pension files after being missed by the published roster. The standard histories missed them. The pension files didn't. That's why we go to the original documents.

The research that uncovered them took months of work in digitized pension records, cross-referenced against muster cards and the company history. Each find is documented in the binder with the source image.

The full memorial roll, with sources, is in the binder. This page will be updated as the research is completed and confirmed.

Noah Jones

Lieutenant Noah Jones led Company A's troopers forward at Bloomery Furnace on 14 February 1862 — the engagement where General Lander called out for the Ohio boys, and they answered. Jones and the men who followed him that day are the reason the motto stuck.

Jones came home from the war. He rests today in Washington Cemetery, Washington Court House, Ohio. We've been to the grave.

His story — his service, his pension file, his postwar life and his burial — is documented in the binder. He is one of the men we know in full because the pension records survived and we went looking.

What the research is

The binder is 96 pages of original-source research. It is not a summary of secondary accounts. Every claim has a source image behind it — a scan of a muster card, a pension file page, a letter home.

When a secondary source says one thing and the primary record says another, the primary record wins. When the record is unclear, we say so. When something is disputed, we present the dispute.

The research started with Company A. It is now moving to Companies C, E and K of the 1st Ohio Volunteer Cavalry — finding the same level of detail for every company, company by company.

Work on Companies C, E and K is ongoing as of late 2026.

Remembering them in the field

Living history is one way of keeping faith with these men. When we ride in period-correct kit, using the right insignia, following the actual regulations — we are saying that the details matter because the men were real and the history is real.

The men of Company A were ordinary Ohioans who signed a three-year paper and rode into a war that killed thirteen of them. The least we can do is get the chevrons pointing the right direction.
