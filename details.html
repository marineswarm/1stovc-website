We go by the book

Our authority is the Revised Army Regulations of 1861. These are the regulations that were actually in effect when Company A mustered in. When something looks wrong in a photograph — when you see chevrons pointing up, or yellow where there shouldn't be yellow — we check the paragraph number before we say anything.

What follows is what the regulations say, with the paragraph numbers so you can look it up yourself.

Cavalry yellow

Yellow — specifically a "golden yellow" — was the branch color of the cavalry throughout the war. It showed up on:

  • Trouser welts. A welt of cavalry yellow ran down the outer seam of the trousers for enlisted men. (Para. 1470)
  • Hat cords. The hat cord on the black felt dress hat was yellow for cavalry. (Para. 1488)
  • Officer shoulder straps. Cavalry officers wore shoulder straps with a yellow ground and a gold border. (Para. 1540)

Yellow as a branch color predates the Civil War. It had been cavalry's color since the army reorganization of 1851.

Paras. 1470, 1488, 1540 of the Revised Army Regulations, 1861.

The crossed sabers

Crossed sabers in brass — the cavalry's branch insignia — were worn on the hat and in some cases on other equipment. The regulations are specific about how they were oriented and how the numbers and letters were placed.

Orientation

Hilts down, edges up. This is not intuitive. Many reproductions get it wrong. The 1861 regulations are clear: the sabers cross with their points up and the hilts at the bottom. (Para. 1485)

The regimental number

The regiment's number — "1" for the 1st Ohio — went in the upper angle of the crossed sabers, between the blades near the tips. (Para. 1485)

The company letter

The company letter — "A" for Company A — was placed above the crossed sabers, in the space above the junction. (Para. 1488)

Guards curling up

The saber guards — the curved metal hand protection below the grip — curl upward in the cross. This is a detail that's easy to miss and equally easy to get wrong in reproductions.

The brass crossed sabers are what you see on our insignia. We had them made from an 1861 reference photograph.

Paras. 1485 and 1488 of the Revised Army Regulations, 1861.

NCO chevrons: points down

This is the one that surprises people most. Modern military chevrons point up. Civil War cavalry chevrons pointed down.

The regulations specify that noncommissioned officers' chevrons were of yellow silk or worsted, worn on both sleeves of the uniform coat, above the elbow, points down. (Para. 1549)

Points up is a 20th-century convention. At Shiloh and Gettysburg, the stripes went the other way. If your sergeant's chevrons are pointing up, the regulations say you've got it backwards.

Sergeant Major

Three bars with a curved arc of three bars — the arc below the straight bars. Points down. Yellow worsted.

First Sergeant

Three bars with a lozenge (diamond). Points down. Yellow worsted.

Sergeant

Three bars. Points down. Yellow worsted.

Corporal

Two bars. Points down. Yellow worsted.

Para. 1549 of the Revised Army Regulations, 1861.

Officer insignia

Officers were distinguished by their shoulder straps, the style of their coats, and their sword and sash.

Captain

Shoulder straps with a yellow ground and gold border, with two gold bars at each end of the strap. (Para. 1543)

First Lieutenant

Shoulder straps with a yellow ground and gold border, with one gold bar at each end. (Para. 1544)

Second Lieutenant

Shoulder straps with a yellow ground and gold border, plain (no bars). (Para. 1545)

Paras. 1543–1545 of the Revised Army Regulations, 1861.

A note on photographs

Period photographs are some of the best evidence we have — but they have limits. Wet-plate collodion photography reversed left and right, so a saber worn on the left hip appears to be on the right. Colors don't show: dark blue and black are indistinguishable. And men cleaned up for portraits in ways they didn't in the field.

We use photographs as corroborating evidence, not as the final word. The regulations say what was prescribed. The photographs show what men actually wore, which wasn't always the same thing.
