About this list

These are the engagements graded OFFICIAL in Appendix E of the regimental history — the ones that count on the company's official service record. We don't pad the list with skirmishes that aren't graded official, and we don't drop anything that is.

Some battles you might expect to find here — Antietam, Hanover, Brandy Station — are listed in the regimental history as Regimental History, Letter or Book grade, not Official. That's what the record says. We go by the record.

Total: 11 official engagements, 1861–1865.

Greenbrier River 3 October 1861 Official

An early engagement in western Virginia, where Union forces under Gen. J.J. Reynolds struck Confederate positions at Camp Bartow on the Greenbrier River. The 1st OVC was part of the Union force in this probe of Confederate defenses in the mountains. Both sides pulled back; the Confederates held their position. It was one of the regiment's first tests under fire.

Western Virginia theater. Company A was present with the regiment.

Kernstown 23 March 1862 Official

Stonewall Jackson attacked a Union force under General Shields south of Winchester, believing he faced only a small rear guard. He was wrong — Shields had the numbers — and Jackson's men were driven back after a hard fight. It was Jackson's only defeat of the war, though it had strategic consequences: Washington, fearing for the Valley, held troops back from McClellan's Peninsula Campaign.

The company history names Companies A and C of the 1st OVC as present at Kernstown.

Source: Company A history and letters home. The engagement is also in the official record.

Front Royal 30 May 1862 Official

Jackson's Confederate forces swept down on the Union garrison at Front Royal in a sudden strike that caught the defenders off guard. The Union force — primarily the 1st Maryland Infantry — was overwhelmed. The 1st OVC was engaged as the Union position in the northern Shenandoah Valley rapidly collapsed. Front Royal opened the door for Jackson's advance on Winchester and his rapid march north.

Valley Campaign, 1862. Part of Jackson's sweep that drove Union forces out of the Valley.

Port Republic 8–9 June 1862 Official

The final engagement of Jackson's Valley Campaign. Jackson's forces defeated the Union commands of Shields and Frémont in quick succession near Port Republic, Virginia, on consecutive days — Shields on the 9th, Frémont on the 8th — ending the Union attempt to trap Jackson in the Valley. With this victory Jackson was free to slip away to join Lee before Richmond.

Port Republic closed out one of the most audacious campaigns of the war. The 1st OVC was present through the fighting retreat as the Union forces withdrew.

Source: official engagement record, Appendix E.

Catlett's Station 22 August 1862 Official

J.E.B. Stuart's Confederate cavalry made a night raid on General Pope's headquarters at Catlett's Station in a driving rainstorm. Stuart's men captured Pope's dispatch book, his dress uniform, and several staff officers — a significant intelligence haul. Company A was serving at this point as escort to General Pope, making this engagement a close one for the men.

The raid was an embarrassment for Pope and gave Lee critical intelligence about Union plans in the days leading up to Second Bull Run.

Source: official engagement record, Appendix E. Company A was detached as Pope's escort at this time.

Second Bull Run 29–30 August 1862 Official

Lee and Jackson crushed Pope's Army of Virginia on the old Manassas battlefield. Jackson's flank march, Longstreet's hammer blow on the Union left — it was a masterpiece of Confederate coordination and a Union disaster. Pope's army fell back toward Washington in disorder. The 1st OVC was in the thick of it and in the retreat that followed.

Second Bull Run effectively ended Pope's command and forced Lincoln to restore McClellan. The war in the East would not go well for the Union for a long time after this.

Source: official engagement record, Appendix E.

Gettysburg 1–3 July 1863 Official

The largest battle ever fought on American soil. Lee's second invasion of the North ended here in three days of brutal fighting. Meade's Army of the Potomac held Cemetery Ridge, repulsed Pickett's Charge on the third day, and Lee withdrew to Virginia.

Where Company A stood at Gettysburg is still disputed in our records. The regiment's presence at the battle is on the official record. The company-level documentation has gaps that our research has not yet closed. We will not guess; we will keep digging.

Open question. The regiment is on the official record. Company A's specific role and position are still being traced.

Monterey Pass 4–5 July 1863 Official

The day after Gettysburg ended, Kilpatrick's cavalry division — including the 1st OVC — struck Lee's retreating column at Monterey Pass in the South Mountain range. It was a night action in rain and fog, cavalry against infantry and wagon trains. The Union troopers captured hundreds of prisoners and a significant section of Lee's supply train, one of the most successful pursuit actions of the war.

Monterey Pass often gets lost in Gettysburg's shadow, but for the cavalry it was a sharp, effective fight.

Source: official engagement record, Appendix E.

Mine Run November–December 1863 Official

Meade's Army of the Potomac moved against Lee's position along Mine Run in central Virginia, hoping to get around the Confederate flank. Lee anticipated the move. When Meade's generals found the Confederate lines too strong to assault, Meade called off the attack and withdrew. No major battle was fought; the campaign ended inconclusively as winter closed in. It would be Grant, not Meade, who finally forced the decisive campaign the following spring.

Source: official engagement record, Appendix E.

Kilpatrick's Raid on Richmond February–March 1864 Official

General Judson Kilpatrick proposed a bold cavalry raid directly into the Confederate capital to free Union prisoners held at Belle Isle and Libby Prison. The plan called for a two-column approach — Kilpatrick from the north, Colonel Ulric Dahlgren from the south. The raid failed. Kilpatrick's column was driven off by Confederate home guard and militia; Dahlgren's column was ambushed, and Dahlgren himself was killed.

The raid's failure, and the controversial papers found on Dahlgren's body allegedly ordering the assassination of Jefferson Davis, made the operation one of the war's most contentious episodes. The 1st OVC was part of Kilpatrick's force.

Source: official engagement record, Appendix E.

Pursuit of Jefferson Davis May 1865 Official

After Lee's surrender at Appomattox on 9 April 1865, Confederate President Jefferson Davis fled south through the Carolinas and into Georgia, hoping to reach the Trans-Mississippi and continue the war. Union cavalry fanned out to find him. Davis was captured near Irwinville, Georgia, on 10 May 1865 by troopers of the 4th Michigan Cavalry — but the 1st OVC was part of the larger pursuit operation that closed the net.

It was one of the last acts of the war in the East. The chase had ended. The regiment's service was nearly done.

Source: official engagement record, Appendix E.

What didn't make the list

A number of actions appear in the regimental history and in letters and memoirs but are not graded Official in Appendix E. These include Antietam, Hanover and Brandy Station. We don't claim them as official engagements, but we don't pretend the men weren't there either. They show up in the letters home and the pension files — and that's its own kind of record.
